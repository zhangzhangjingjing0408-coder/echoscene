## Design Context

### Users

EchoScene serves university students who can understand English AI and technology content on platforms such as YouTube, but lack opportunities to actively retell ideas, express opinions, and receive immediate, context-aware feedback.

The primary job is to turn passive video consumption into a focused speaking practice loop: understand the source, receive a grounded task, speak in real time, get targeted help or feedback, and retry one specific weakness without losing the source context.

### Brand Personality

Calm, encouraging, and intellectually confident.

EchoScene should feel like a thoughtful learning companion rather than an evaluator, chatbot dashboard, or attention-seeking AI demo. Its language should be concise, specific, supportive, and adult-to-adult. It should create focus and confidence without becoming childish or overly congratulatory.

### Aesthetic Direction

- Reference the restrained, learning-first visual approach of Zara Zhang's YouTube Digest Chrome side panel: content stays close to the YouTube context, hierarchy is clear, and the interface behaves like a focused companion rather than a separate destination.
- Follow YouTube's current light or dark appearance so the side panel feels native beside the video. Both themes are required; neither is treated as an afterthought.
- Favor an editorial learning-tool aesthetic: readable typography, quiet surfaces, precise hierarchy, compact controls, and visible evidence from the source video.
- Make voice state, task progress, source evidence, and the current coaching focus visually legible without decorative spectacle.
- Explicitly avoid purple-blue gradients, neon waveforms, glowing dark-mode accents, glassmorphism, and grids of colorful generic AI cards.
- Avoid making the product resemble a call-center console, analytics dashboard, language-learning game for children, or generic chatbot.

### Design Principles

1. Keep the learner anchored to the source: important feedback and tasks should connect back to video evidence and timestamps.
2. Make the next action obvious: at any moment, the learner should know whether to listen, speak, retry, review, or return to the video.
3. Visualize state, not decoration: motion and audio visualization must clarify listening, thinking, speaking, interruption, or completion.
4. Reduce performance anxiety: feedback is specific and honest, but the interface remains calm and non-judgmental.
5. Preserve depth in a narrow panel: use progressive disclosure and strong typography instead of nested cards and dense dashboards.
6. Treat accessibility as a baseline: keyboard access, visible focus, reduced-motion support, sufficient contrast, and text alternatives for audio state are required.
